Desire coding

Desire coding is the practice of end users rapidly building their own software with AI assistance, understood as a desire path: a worn trail that reveals unmet need, not a substitute for professional engineering.

It's a signal, not a vibe

Vibe coding describes the mood of the person typing. It says nothing about what the resulting software is for, or what anyone should do with it. Desire coding names the signal. When someone with no engineering training builds a tool to route around non-existent or ineffective software, they have worn a path across the lawn. The path is evidence that shows where the designed route did not go.

The metaphor

Planners learned long ago to read desire paths before pouring concrete. The same discipline applies to software. User-built prototypes are living requirements. They show professional developers exactly where demand exists, what shape it takes, and which parts of the current system people are willing to abandon. That is good news for classical engineering, not a threat to it.

A worn path is not a paved one. Most desire-coded software should never ship as built; the security record on AI-generated code makes that plain. Reading the path is the user's contribution. Deciding whether to pave it, reroute it, or fence it off remains the engineer's job.

Lineage

The metaphor already lives in programming. Larry Wall described Perl as the sidewalks worn into the grass at UC Irvine, formalized after the fact. The HTML5 design principles told standards authors to pave the cowpaths. Desire coding extends that tradition to the moment when the people wearing the paths can also lay their own gravel.
